## Proposed Solution
To resolve this research-level problem posed by Paul Erdős (Problem #1106), we will address both questions in turn. We begin with a formal definition of the objects in question.

### Definitions and Setup

Let $p(n)$ denote the number of unrestricted partitions of $n$. We are interested in the product of the first $n$ partition numbers, which we denote by $P_n$:
$$P_n = \prod_{k=1}^n p(k)$$
Let $\omega(N)$ denote the number of distinct prime factors of an integer $N$. The function $F(n)$ is defined as:
$$F(n) = \omega(P_n)$$
We are asked to prove whether $F(n) \to \infty$ as $n \to \infty$, and to resolve whether $F(n) > n$ for all sufficiently large $n$.

---

### Part I: Proof that $F(n) \to \infty$

**Theorem 1:** $\lim_{n \to \infty} F(n) = \infty$.

**Proof:**
While historically Erdős and Ivić sketched a proof of this using Tijdeman’s theorem on the gaps between $S$-units, a much more direct, powerful, and unconditional proof is available today using modern results on the arithmetic properties of the partition function. 

In 2000, Ken Ono proved a celebrated theorem in the theory of modular forms regarding the divisibility of $p(n)$:
> **Ono's Theorem (Annals of Math, 2000):** For every prime $q \geq 5$, there exist infinitely many integers $n$ such that $p(n) \equiv 0 \pmod{q}$. 

We can trivially cover the remaining primes $q \in \{2, 3\}$ by observing that $p(2) = 2$ and $p(3) = 3$. Therefore, **every single prime number divides $p(n)$ for some $n \ge 1$**.

Let $M$ be any arbitrarily large positive integer, and let $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_M$ be the first $M$ prime numbers. By Ono's Theorem (and the trivial observations for $2$ and $3$), for each prime $q_i$, there exists at least one integer $N_i$ such that $q_i \mid p(N_i)$. 

Let $N = \max_{1 \leq i \leq M} \{N_i\}$. For any $n \geq N$, the product $P_n = \prod_{k=1}^n p(k)$ contains $p(N_i)$ as a factor for every $i \in \{1, \dots, M\}$. Consequently, $q_i \mid P_n$ for all $1 \leq i \leq M$. 

This implies that for all $n \geq N$, $P_n$ has at least $M$ distinct prime factors. Thus, $F(n) \geq M$. Since $M$ was chosen arbitrarily, we rigorously conclude that:
$$F(n) \to \infty \quad \text{as} \quad n \to \infty$$ 
This definitively resolves the first part of the problem. $\blacksquare$

### Part II: Is $F(n) > n$ for all sufficiently large $n$?

The question of whether $F(n) > n$ for all sufficiently large $n$ remains an **open problem** in number theory. However, we can provide a rigorous mathematical analysis—establishing the maximal theoretical bounds and utilizing analytic probabilistic models—to demonstrate that it is overwhelmingly likely to be **TRUE**, explaining exactly why Erdős posited the conjecture. 

#### 1. Rigorous Upper Bound on $F(n)$
First, one must ask if the size of the product $P_n$ is mathematically large enough to even support $>n$ distinct prime factors. 
By the Hardy-Ramanujan asymptotic formula, $\log p(k) \sim C \sqrt{k}$, where $C = \pi \sqrt{2/3}$. Therefore, the natural logarithm of our product $P_n$ is:
$$\log P_n = \sum_{k=1}^n \log p(k) \sim \sum_{k=1}^n C \sqrt{k} \sim \frac{2C}{3} n^{3/2}$$
For any arbitrarily large integer $N$, the maximum number of distinct prime factors it can possess is bounded by the Primorial growth rate:
$$\omega(N) \leq \frac{\log N}{\log \log N} (1 + o(1))$$
Substituting $N = P_n$, we find $\log \log P_n \sim \log(n^{3/2}) = \frac{3}{2}\log n$. Thus, the absolute maximum theoretical limit for $F(n)$ is:
$$F(n) \leq \frac{\frac{2C}{3} n^{3/2}}{\frac{3}{2}\log n} \sim \frac{4\pi \sqrt{2/3}}{9} \frac{n^{3/2}}{\log n} \approx 1.14 \frac{n^{3/2}}{\log n}$$
Because $n^{3/2}/\log n$ grows strictly faster than $n$, the inequality $F(n) > n$ is mathematically permissible. 

#### 2. The Probabilistic Divisibility Heuristic
To evaluate the *expected* behavior of $F(n)$, we model the values $p(k)$ as pseudo-random integers uniformly distributed up to their expected size $e^{C\sqrt{k}}$. While $p(k)$ coefficients are governed by modular forms, for sufficiently large prime moduli $q \gg 1$, their residues become uniformly distributed (analogous to Sato-Tate distributions). 

Assuming independence, the probability that a prime $q$ divides $p(k)$ is $1/q$. The probability that $q$ does **not** divide the product $P_n$ is:
$$\mathbb{P}(q \nmid P_n) = \prod_{k=1}^n \left(1 - \frac{1}{q}\right) \approx e^{-n/q}$$
By linearity of expectation, the expected number of distinct prime factors $E[F(n)]$ is the sum of the probabilities that each prime $q$ divides $P_n$. The maximum available prime factor size is $p(n) \approx e^{C\sqrt{n}}$. Thus:
$$E[F(n)] \approx \sum_{q \leq e^{C\sqrt{n}}} \left(1 - e^{-n/q}\right)$$
We split this sum into two intervals: primes $q \leq n$, and primes $q > n$.
* **For $q \leq n$:** $e^{-n/q}$ is negligibly small (or bounded away from 1), meaning almost all primes up to $n$ will divide the product. This contributes $\sim \pi(n) \sim \frac{n}{\log n}$ primes.
* **For $q > n$:** Using the Taylor expansion $1 - e^{-n/q} \approx \frac{n}{q}$, the contribution from large primes is evaluated using Mertens' Second Theorem:
$$\sum_{n < q \leq e^{C\sqrt{n}}} \frac{n}{q} = n \left( \sum_{q \leq e^{C\sqrt{n}}} \frac{1}{q} - \sum_{q \leq n} \frac{1}{q} \right) \approx n \Big( \log \log(e^{C\sqrt{n}}) - \log \log n \Big)$$
$$= n \Big( \log(C\sqrt{n}) - \log \log n \Big) = n \left( \frac{1}{2} \log n + \log C - \log \log n \right) \sim \frac{1}{2} n \log n$$

Combining both regimes, we find that the expected number of distinct prime factors is:
$$E[F(n)] \sim \frac{1}{2} n \log n$$

Because the expected growth $\frac{1}{2} n \log n$ dominates $n$ for sufficiently large $n$ (specifically $n \geq e^2$), the probabilistic framework heavily indicates that **$F(n) > n$ is TRUE for all sufficiently large $n$**. 

However, passing from this heuristic to a rigorous unconditional proof would require deep, currently unknown bounds on the uniform distribution of $p(n) \pmod q$ acting simultaneously over moving bounds of primes $q \sim n$. As such, while the affirmative answer is overwhelmingly anticipated, an unconditional proof of $F(n) > n$ remains out of reach for modern mathematics, fulfilling its status as an enduring Open Problem.
